### Briggs's Bingo Tracking Speed Exploration
For one of the final shots of the [Why is our project important video notes & script](Why%20is%20our%20project%20important%20video%20notes%20&%20script.md) video, we had to fake the auto-tracking on Bingo. This was for two reasons:
1) The "Speed" slider in Commander only applied to manual jogs, not auto-track
2) Even if that slider worked, it would have probably led to more problems than it solved

The latter is because the "Speed" slider affected *all* axes. That sounds like it'd be what you want, but it actually isn't; we just wanted Bingo to follow Aleck faster, which means all that was needed was angular velocity of panning - that is, base rotation speed. When other axes are faster, tiny incorrect adjustments snowball into massive, constant overcorrections, which would be downright nauseating to watch in recorded format. We want to avoid those at all costs. That is to say, we want to minimize **overshoot** and **hunting**[^1].

In my testing on Monday (yesterday, 2026-11-16), I found two main causes of overshoot/hunting. One was speed, as mentioned above, and the other was centering tolerance. Let's delve into them both.

(also, for the record, i'm a human writing this. i know i write kinda like claude, but i promise this is just how i am)

#### Speed
The first finding was that physical, observed movement speed does not scale linearly with `SPEED`; at 20% the arm runs about 25% faster than a straight scale-down from 100% predicts.

That probably doesn't really matter for us? But I figured it'd be useful to jot down in case it's relevant to anyone in the future.

The more important thing is that speed *itself* can cause faults.

Take the elbow joint, for instance. Run it at 50% speed and ask it to move upwards, and it'll *probably* fault. With my changes, those faults don't happen, but not because I waved a magic wand and fixed anything. The problem here is one that's quite literally inescapable: gravity. The motors can't dish out enough current/voltage to fight against it at such a speed, so they effectively stall out, causing the controller to yell at us.

And speed can cause non-fault/impact problems, too, which links back to the overshoot/hunting thing. Take the wrist pitch movement speed, for instance. If you move the wrist at 50% speed, auto-track *will* overshoot the subject and end up pointed at the ceiling sooner or later. Probably pretty quickly. This is because it's the most sensitive joint: **at the same SPEED, pitch turns 2-6x faster in degrees per second**, so a relatively small change in count results in a massive change in location, and that change in location has an *even more massive* change in what's visible on screen - often, the subject isn't, and the track gets lost. In the future this could get fixed by reacquisition, but why let it be a problem in the first place? If you just try 50% wrist speed, you'll quickly realize that it is far, *far* too fast.

So I introduced soft speed limits in Pi-Vision on `feat/travel-budget`. Not 'soft' like real-life speed limits that people ignore, but rather 'soft' as in "you can modify these with environment variables, but you probably shouldn't unless you're testing something specific". In particular:

- `ELBOW_MAX_SPEED` defaults to 30%, AKA `ELBOW_MAX_SPEED=30`, to avoid fighting gravity
- `SHOULDER_MAX_SPEED` defaults to 30% as well
- `PITCH_MAX_SPEED` defaults to 20%, to avoid overshooting since tiny adjustments in the motors lead to massive changes in what's visible to the camera
- `BASE_MAX_SPEED` is **not** capped by default. it can reasonably be driven at even 70% or more, depending on how far away your subject is, simply due to the 3d geometry of what its movements are accomplishing for the camera's view

If you want to implement a max speed for base rotation, then, just set the environment variable appropriately, e.g. `BASE_MAX_SPEED=10` if you want the base to slow to a crawl. You can set this in Gaia by going to the Settings menu at the top right, scrolling to Pi-Vision's section, and writing the `NAME=value` onto a new line.

> [!info] tl;dr
> I've made Commander's speed slider affect auto-track *(admittedly in a separate pi-vision branch - will merge later)*, but **it softmaxes elbow and shoulder to 30% and pitch to 20%**. Only base rotation gets the full value.

#### Centering Tolerance/Acceptable Box Radius
Note that pitch overshoot isn't actually fixed by the speed limit. Not entirely. It's just far less dramatic when it's slowed down (far less likely to end up looking at the ceiling). The only real fix for the constant wrist pitch changes/hunting would have to involve the centering tolerance.

Speaking of centering tolerance: Pi-Vision chooses from various possible points when picking what it counts as "being in the box", and if it's jumping between those points and none of them can feasibly fit in the box at the same time, you've got a problem. As such, one should tune centering tolerance carefully depending on the typical distance of their subject (and by extension the size of their face within the frame).

#### Bingo's Limits
I also tested to find Bingo's soft limits in terms of robot position on Monday 11/16, and found these which felt safe:

| Joint    | Min   | Max  | Note                                          |
| -------- | ----- | ---- | --------------------------------------------- |
| base     | −4500 | 4500 | the controller itself stops it at about -5100 |
| shoulder | −150  | 2000 |                                               |
| elbow    | −1300 | 1000 | the real maximum depends on the shoulder      |
| pitch    | −50   | 1000 |                                               |

The note for elbow is important btw! I have not yet performed a full test of tons of different shoulder positions to essentially find some mathematical relationship between elbow min/max and current shoulder position. That's left as future work atm. I tried to use safe numbers here though, so hopefully this should minimize faults in the field.

Pro tip: if you are having faults/impacts during auto-track, then after clearing the fault you should probably manually jog the robot to a better position. And not just one tap in the opposite direction that it faulted from, either. Find some other way to get the subject inside that "acceptable box" area, or at least close enough to it that auto-track can find a more sustainable way to finish the job. (I say this mostly for future teams - we can probably already intuit this ourselves, but for newcomers it might ease their onboarding experience a little!)

Oh, but be warned: only auto-track applies these speed limits. Manual tracks still go as fast as whatever speed you've set in Commander. Here be dragons! Be careful!

#### Motion-Aware Polling
You might be wondering why I was messing with all this. Good question. The short answer is that I'm trying to make tracking *feel* responsive. The easy way of doing that is shrinking the centering tolerance, but that has its own problems (as discussed above). The harder way of doing that is by allowing ourselves to send more commands to the robot… but how? After all, the serial line is busy ~80% of the time *when the arm is idle*. That's mostly due to telemetry.

Now, I respect telemetry. It's useful. I put in work to find limits, after all — limits that are only useful if you can compare Bingo's current position with them. Doing that requires telemetry. Telemetry is important, and we can't just go ripping it out.

But we sure do ask for it **often**, don't we?

Enter: "Motion-Aware Polling", available on `feat/motion-aware-polling` in Operator and `feat/travel-budget` in Pi-Vision. 

The problem, in short, was that many jogs felt sluggish because they had to wait for a telemetry poll. Doing a `LISTPV POSITION` call takes about 390ms and *cannot* be interrupted, so if a jog arrives while the controller is figuring out where its joints are, it has to wait *up to a third of a second* before it can actually be executed. Which felt kind of slow, especially in our small team room.

And, for long movements (since we can't get telemetry *during* a movement), joint limit checks were being skipped entirely after 1.25s. That's bad. Dangerous, even.

The solution to both of these problems was, unfortunately, math. In `feat/travel-budget`, Pi-Vision takes the most recent telemetry reading then models the worst-case travel of every move that it's commanded since. You can think of it kind of like a 'true' digital twin; we're making guesses about where the robot's position is for the purpose of getting information that we otherwise wouldn't have access to. But it's all physically-backed information based on real measurements.

Along the way, I found some oddities. The existing base and elbow limit checks pointed the wrong way on Bingo, the base check never stopped a move already running, and Bingo had no soft limits configured for shoulder, elbow or pitch. Just rotation, at +/- 5000, but I've gotten Bingo to over +6800 before, and I've never gotten it *lower* than -5170. The controller caps out at the latter, and the webcam cable caps out at the former.

In short: the new version polls every 900ms when idle (busy about 45% of the time instead of ~80%), and waits for a "quiet window" after each jog before trying to poll again in case you send another jog. I've set that quiet window to 1.5 seconds on this branch.

> [!info] But is it actually faster?
> Yes, kind of. Mostly.
> 
> Here are some graphs, then let me explain:
> ![624](2026_11_17-briggs-quiet-window-testing.png)
> The first graph is a comparison between no quiet window, 650ms quiet window, and 1.5s quiet window. By waiting longer and polling less often, fewer jog starts are blocked by a poll, and jog starts happen quicker. At 1.5s, it never once lost a jog.
> 
> It *does* have downsides, however. Namely:
> - Future predictive motion efforts might make this obsolete, since we could intelligently plan polling based on our predictions or something along those lines.
> 	- If we go to creating pre-planned moves on the fly then executing them, we can actually get telemetry during movement, which also negates this, though that approach hasn't been tested afaik.
> - **Four unnecessary limit stops in 7.5 min of tracking.** Pi-Vision halted the base for "base would reach its limit" four times, but the base never went past about ±2650 counts, well short of the ±4500 limit. With fewer position readings, Pi-Vision's estimate of how far the arm might have moved grows until it hits the limit. Each stop costs roughly 2s until the next reading arrives. The original code had one such stop.
> 	- This is theoretically mendable by making pi-vision ask for a reading early when an estimate gets close to its limit, but I haven't implemented/tested that yet.
> - **Fewer position readings:** about 23 per minute while tracking, against 67 for the original code. Anything that shows the arm's live position, like Commander, updates less often. Terrible news for digital twin fans.
> - **Small sample size:** I did one real auto-track run per variant with about 60 tracking starts each. The scripted run is the strong evidence because every variant got identical input. The auto-track numbers *do* point the same way, but they're way noisier.
> 
> So it does seem to be mathematically better, but honestly not by as much as I'd've hoped. I'm not sure if I'd notice the difference in the real world.
> 
> But my biggest worry about these changes is actually Bluey. **Would these changes mess with Bluey in any way?** I honestly have no idea, which is why I want to present this in detail. Operator just skips telemetry for Bluey right now, and I don't know how it's planned to be implemented in the future. I also don't know if Bluey has similar speed/position limits, for whatever it's worth.
> 
> So let me know if any of this needs to be worked on to fit with Bluey. And I don't think we should even *think* about merging this until after MakerFaire either way.



[^1]: **hysteresis** is typically the *fix* to these problems (it means different thresholds for entering and leaving a state). we could add some hysteresis to the acceptable bounding box if we wanted, to minimize overshoot/hunting while still allowing for smaller boxes
